# The Bureaucratic Journey

The long path through the pension system is the real story of this file. Wilkins spent at least twenty years petitioning, explaining, swearing, and being doubted.

His 1892 claim opened with his own affidavit and testimony from neighbors. He said he suffered from “misery in head and jaw and back ach from carrying Cartridges during the war,” and declared his capacity for labor was only a quarter that of an able-bodied man. Two witnesses, John Logwood and Moses Green, swore that they knew him and believed him unable to do more than “1/4” of an able-bodied man’s work.

But the medical examiners were not convinced. In October 1892, a surgeon’s certificate acknowledged some real physical problems but reduced them to a narrow rating. The examination found no jaw disease, noted decayed teeth, hemorrhoids “to size of cranberry,” and “Complete loss of Lattiral movement of both Great Toes.” The examiner thought his “General appearance Excellent” and recommended a small rating only for the toes.

In the years that followed, Wilkins kept adding detail, trying to fit his suffering into a system that preferred precise, documentable injuries. In 1895 he swore that his toe injury had occurred in April 1866. In 1896 he said a U.S. surgeon had pulled two teeth at once and laid him up in the hospital for a month. The War Department, however, found only brief treatment for catarrh and diarrhea. The gap between lived experience and surviving official record worked against him.

In 1904 he filed another claim under the 1890 law. This time he described chronic weakness, senility, injury to his foot, and the strain of military labor. The Bureau sent him for a medical examination in November 1905. That examination is one of the most revealing documents in the file because it both validates and minimizes him. The board found no evidence of the right-foot injury he described, some deformity in the left foot “due to ill fitting shoe,” heart enlargement, an arm scar, and most importantly a rectal disorder:

> “Piles There are now no active tumors. but applicant
> has fistulous opening 3 inches to left of anus, which
> causes much discomfort”

The examining surgeons recommended a modest rate—**eight dollars per month**—for “disease of rectum.” The Medical Division even conceded in November 1905 that he was “entitled to a rate under act June 27, 1890 for disease of rectum.” But then came the bureaucratic trap: the Bureau insisted that it was **not shown that such disability existed at date of filing claim** in August 1904.

That timing issue haunted the claim. Wilkins and his attorneys tried to bridge the gap with affidavits and a doctor’s statement. In 1906 he swore that “some years ago” he had been pronounced by Dr. Dodge to have “some disease of Rectum” and that he had grown steadily worse until he could no longer do hard manual labor. Dr. E. G. Martin certified in November 1906 that Wilkins was suffering from “internal hemorroids of medium size, slightly inflamed.” But the Bureau replied coldly that this only showed his condition in 1906, not in 1904, and that unsworn witness statements could not be accepted.

His attorneys argued that if he had rectal disease in 1892 and again in 1905, it was reasonable to infer the disability existed in 1904. The Medical Division rejected that logic. In a 1908 memorandum, it stated that the hemorrhoid seen in 1892 was “certainly not severe” and that in a condition as variable as piles, an examination from twelve years earlier had “little or no bearing” on his condition at the filing date. The claim was rejected in March 1908 for “insufficiency of evidence to show a pensionable disability since date of filing claim.”

That same year, however, the law changed in his favor. Instead of proving disability, Wilkins could claim by age. He filed in January 1908 under the **Act of February 6, 1907**, stating he was 62. The Bureau actually found evidence showing him older—64—and admitted the claim, awarding **$12 per month**. This was a profound turning point: after years of trying to convince examiners that his body had been damaged enough, he finally qualified by surviving into old age.

Even then the struggle did not entirely end. In 1910 he filed for an increase, claiming to be 70. The Bureau rejected the request because “it is apparent that he… cannot prove that he was 70 years of age.” Again, the uncertainty imposed by slavery and poor recordkeeping became an obstacle. Only under the 1912 law did he secure another increase. The Bureau asked the Adjutant General for his age at enlistment; the army record gave 20. From that, the Bureau fixed his date of birth as **July 27, 1843**, and approved the higher rate in 1913.

# Historical Notes

This file reveals much about the experience of Black veterans in the postwar pension system.

First, it shows how freedom did not bring immediate documentary security. Wilkins had to prove his age, identity, service, disability, marriage, and family relationships again and again. But the records of his birth and early life were shaped by slavery, where such information was not kept for his benefit. His name appeared in several forms in military records; his age shifted in different applications; even his regiment was recorded inconsistently in places. None of this was unusual for formerly enslaved veterans, but all of it could be used against them.

Second, the file highlights the central role of local Black community testimony. Neighbors, fellow residents, and family acquaintances repeatedly stepped forward to say they knew him, knew his suffering, and knew his identity. In the absence of abundant formal documentation, Black claimants relied on memory networks—people who had seen them work, decline, marry, and endure.

Third, it shows how the Bureau’s standards could be exacting in ways that disadvantaged poor rural claimants. Wilkins’s disability may well have been real and substantial, but the claim failed because he could not prove the exact degree of disability on the precise filing date. The Bureau could acknowledge that he suffered from a ratable rectal disease in 1905 and still deny the claim because no one had preserved acceptable evidence for August 1904.

Finally, the widow correspondence from 1927 to 1930 shows how pension law regulated intimate life long after the war. A widow’s entire claim could turn on a marriage date. Sallie Abbs may have been poor and dependent, but because she married Wilkins in 1926 instead of before June 27, 1905, she was excluded. The law favored older, documentable marriages and punished those whose unions fell outside bureaucratic eligibility.

Taken together, the file of Wilkins Abbs is not just a pension record. It is the paper trail of a Black veteran’s long struggle to make the federal government recognize a life that slavery, war, and poverty had rendered hard to document but not hard to feel. He survived the war, farmed for decades, raised a family, and spent much of his later life asking the state to acknowledge what service had cost him. Eventually it did—but only partially, and only after years of insistence.
