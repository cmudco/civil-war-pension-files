# The Pension File of Wilkins Abbs: A Life in Documents

## Background

Wilkins Abbs was born in Wilkinson County, Mississippi, in or around 1843—though the precise year of his birth would become a matter of considerable bureaucratic dispute over the decades that followed. Various documents in his file give his birth year as 1840, 1843, 1846, or 1848, a discrepancy that was not unusual for formerly enslaved people who had no birth certificates and whose ages were recorded, if at all, only in the ledgers of their enslavers. The military records listed his age as 20 at enlistment in 1863, which would place his birth around 1843. Abbs himself could not write, signing all documents with an "X" mark throughout his life—a fact noted repeatedly by the Pension Bureau clerks who processed his claims.

Before the war, Abbs was enslaved. When asked about his former owners, he named "Douglass Cooper." He worked as a laborer in Wilkinson County, in the southwestern corner of Mississippi, near the Louisiana border. The War Department's efforts to identify his former owner through military records came up empty: "name of owner has not been found," the Adjutant General reported in 1908. The details of his enslaved life remain, as for so many, largely invisible in the documentary record.

## Military Service

Wilkins Abbs enlisted on July 27, 1863, at Vicksburg, Mississippi—the city that had fallen to Union forces just weeks earlier, on July 4, opening the Mississippi River and fundamentally altering the war's trajectory. Like thousands of formerly enslaved men in the lower Mississippi Valley, Abbs seized the opportunity to join the Union Army. He was approximately twenty years old, stood five feet seven inches tall, with a black complexion, black eyes, and black hair.

His military service involved a complicated series of unit redesignations that reflected the evolving bureaucratic structure of the United States Colored Troops. He was initially assigned to Company I, 1st Louisiana Colored Infantry, African Descent, which was later redesignated as the 9th Louisiana Infantry (Volunteers of African Descent), then reorganized as the 1st Mississippi Colored Heavy Artillery, before finally becoming Company I, 5th United States Colored Heavy Artillery. Through all these name changes, Abbs served in the same company.

The regiment was stationed primarily at Vicksburg, and the records show Abbs present with his unit through most of the war. In February 1864, he was briefly detailed on recruiting service—likely working to bring other Black men into the Union Army. That same month, he was treated for catarrh (a respiratory infection) from February 4 to 7, 1864, and again in June 1864 for diarrhea, spending two days in the hospital. These were common ailments among Civil War soldiers, particularly those stationed in the damp, disease-ridden Mississippi River valley.

In later affidavits, Abbs described his wartime experience in more vivid terms than the dry military rolls could convey. He told of carrying forty rounds of cartridges and other heavy equipment as a young man, work that he believed damaged his spine and caused lasting back pain. He recalled that a U.S. Army surgeon "pulled Two teeth at one time and layed me up in hospital one month in Vicksburg." And near the end of the war, while serving as a provost guard in Vicksburg, he described falling into a hole while pursuing "malefactors," seriously injuring his right foot.

He was honorably discharged as a Private on May 20, 1866, having served approximately two years and nine months.

## The Pension Claim

Wilkins Abbs's engagement with the federal pension system spanned over three decades, beginning in 1892 and continuing through successive acts of Congress until his death in 1927. His story illustrates the exhausting, often Sisyphean experience of Black veterans navigating a bureaucracy that demanded documentary proof from people whose lives had been deliberately kept unrecorded.

### The First Claim (1892)

On July 22, 1892, Abbs filed his first pension application under the Act of June 27, 1890, which provided pensions to Union veterans who were unable to earn a support by manual labor, regardless of whether their disability was service-connected. He was living on the "Isola Plantation" in Bolivar County, Mississippi, and described himself as suffering from "misery in the Head & jaw bones from passing forth during the war." He was represented by the Washington law firm of Tucker, Evans & Manogue.

A supplementary declaration filed on September 1, 1892, added "back ache" to his list of ailments, and on August 26, 1895, he alleged injury to both great toes.

### The Medical Examinations

Abbs was examined by a Board of Surgeons at Bellevue, Mississippi, on October 12, 1892. The board found:

- A "slight indentation of right Sub. Maxillary bone immediately under 2nd Molar Tooth"
- "Many decayed teeth which I think causes Neuralgia in head"
- "Hemroids protrude to size of cranberry easily replaced. Not bleeding."
- "Complete loss of Lattiral movement of both Great Toes"
- "Much Lumbago Pain when stooping"
- "General apperance Excellent"

The board recommended a rating of one-quarter disability and suggested $8 per month for "Anchylosis Of Both Toes." His overall physical appearance was deemed "Excellent"—a finding that would work against him.

A second, more thorough examination came on November 1, 1905, conducted at Greenville, Mississippi, by Drs. S.R. Dunn, Owen W. Stone, and E.C. Smythe. This examination was more detailed and more revealing:

- Pulse rate elevated: 90 sitting, 96 standing, 104 after exercise
- Temperature slightly elevated at 99 degrees
- Heart: "action accelerated, with prolonged first sound... some hypertrophy, with dilatation"
- A scar five inches long on his right arm, "adherent at one point and is dragging, evidently impairing strength of arm"
- A fistulous opening three inches to the left of the anus, "which causes much discomfort"
- No active hemorrhoid tumors but evidence of previous fistulous openings
- "applicant uses tobacco to excess"
- No evidence of respiratory trouble, syphilis, or "vicious habits"
- The board recommended a rating of $8 per month

Despite this examination showing real disabilities, the case continued to founder on a bureaucratic technicality.

## The Bureaucratic Journey

### Repeated Rejections (1892–1908)

The first claim under the Act of June 27, 1890, was rejected on August 12, 1904—twelve years after it was filed—"on the ground of insufficiency of the evidence to show a pensionable degree of disability from date of filing." This was the Bureau's way of saying that while Abbs might have had disabilities, they could not confirm he was disabled enough *at the specific moment* he filed his claim to qualify.

The Medical Division's internal notes reveal the frustrating circularity of the Bureau's reasoning. In November 1905, the Medical Division acknowledged that the 1905 examination "shows claimant entitled to a rate under act June 27, 1890 for disease of rectum," but added the devastating caveat: "From a medical standpoint it is not shown that such disability existed at date of filing claim." The claim had been filed in 1904; the examination occurred in 1905. Because more than a year had elapsed between filing and examination, the Bureau refused to assume the disease existed at the earlier date.

An internal note, likely from a sympathetic clerk, observed:

> "Severe protruding Piles are shown on Examination in 1892, No operation for their removal appears to have been had. It seems that the testimony of claimants Corroborated by 2 neighbors ought to be sufficient in court, especially as there is a record of diarrhoea."

This note captures the tension within the Bureau itself—some officials recognized the absurdity of demanding proof that a chronic condition existed on a specific date when no doctor had been available to examine the claimant on that date.

Abbs's attorneys, William Fletcher & Co., argued forcefully for reopening the case in March 1908:

> "It appears that when this applicant was examined Oct. 12, 1892, he then had severe disease of rectum, and the Board of Surgeons that examined him Nov. 1, 1905 appears to have described a ratable degree of disability by reason of same, and it is certainly most reasonable to suppose that this disability existed in a pensionable degree at the date of filing claim."

The Bureau was unmoved. The Assistant Medical Referee, Addison S. Hellon, responded that the 1892 examination showed only "a pile protruding the size of a cranberry which was easily replaced, a condition that is certainly not severe," and that "in a disability which is so varying in nature and degree as piles, an examination made nearly twelve years before the claim in question was filed could have little or no bearing on the question as to the claimant's condition at the date of filing his claim."

The claim under the Act of June 27, 1890, was definitively rejected on March 4, 1908.

### Success Under the Act of February 6, 1907

By this time, however, Congress had passed the Act of February 6, 1907, which provided age-based pensions to Civil War veterans. On January 28, 1908, Abbs filed a new declaration under this act, claiming he was 62 years old (born 1846). The evidence showed him to be about 64. On March 6, 1908, he was finally granted a pension of $12 per month—his first pension payment after sixteen years of trying.

### The Age Dispute and Attempted Increase (1910)

Emboldened by his success, Abbs filed for an increase on March 9, 1910, now claiming to be 70 years old (born 1840), which would have entitled him to a higher rate. The Bureau rejected this claim flatly on March 31, 1910: "the evidence does not show and it is apparent that he, Claimant, cannot prove that he was 70 years of age at the time of executing the pending claim, hence he is not entitled to a greater rate of pension under the act of February 6, 1907, than he is now in receipt of thereunder."

The Bureau's file tracking his birth year tells the story in miniature: at various times, Abbs or his representatives claimed he was born in 1840, 1843, 1846, or 1848. The military records from 1863 said he was 20, placing his birth around 1843. But Abbs, like most formerly enslaved people, genuinely did not know his exact birth date. When asked on the 1915 questionnaire for his date of birth, he gave "July 27"—the same date as his enlistment. Whether this was his actual birthday, a date assigned to him by the Army, or simply the only significant date he could remember, the record does not reveal.

### The Act of May 11, 1912, and Subsequent Increases

When Congress passed the Act of May 11, 1912, providing more generous age-based pensions, Abbs filed again on July 1, 1912. The Bureau inquired with the War Department about his age at enlistment and was told he was listed as 20 years old, which would make him born around 1843. On May 13, 1913, his pension was approved at $18 per month, retroactive to July 1, 1912, with his date of birth set at July 27, 1843, making him approximately 68 years old.

His pension was subsequently increased to $24 per month effective July 27, 1913 (his 70th birthday under the Bureau's calculation), and to $30 per month effective July 27, 1918 (his 75th birthday). By the time of his death, he was receiving $65 per month—a substantial sum that reflected both his advanced age and the progressive liberalization of Civil War pension rates.

## Voices from the File

The pension file preserves the voices of Wilkins Abbs and his community in remarkable detail. In his 1904 affidavit, Abbs described his wartime injury with a specificity that leaps off the page:

> "A short while before I was discharged in Vicksburg Miss. one night while serving on duty as 'Provost Guard' in attempting to arrest some malefactors And while running after them I fall in a Hole and seriously hurt my right foot I was mustered out in a few days after and had to go under medical attention after I was discharged. my foot has been weak and painful ever since."

In 1896, he recalled the heavy burden of military service on his young body:

> "on or about Jany 1865 in Vicksburg I was Carrying 40 rounds of Cartridges and Other Equipments. being young it injured my spine. Causing Constant misery in the back, hurt my toes in the same place on rocks while on duty. U S Surgeon pulled Two teeth at one time and layed me up in hospital one month in Vicksburg."

In 1906, Abbs tried to explain the chronic nature of his suffering in terms the Bureau might accept:

> "Some years ago, - I think - in 1879 or 1890 - most certainly some years before the year 1904 - I became incapacitated for hard work (I am a farmer) I was examined by Dr Dodge of Bolivar who pronounced my disease - some disease of Rectum - He treated me for it - I have grown worse and am now unable to do hard manual labor. - I am now living with my son - and I have ceased to try to farm myself as I am unable to do the work."

His neighbors testified on his behalf repeatedly. In 1892, John Logwood and Moses Green—men who had known him for sixteen years—swore that he suffered from "misery of head and jaw and back ach." Charles H. Scott, age 56, and O.O. Lewis, age 40, testified in 1906 that they had known Abbs "for more than twenty years and of our personal knowledge know his statement above to be true."

Dr. E.G. Martin of Benoit, Mississippi, provided medical testimony in November 1906 that Abbs was "suffering from internal hemorroids of medium size, slightly inflamed but not ulcerated. They do not protrude except on straining at stool or on speculum examination."

## Family and Personal Life

Wilkins Abbs married Elizabeth Hopkins on January 14, 1879, at Milliken's Bend, Louisiana, with Reverend Stable White officiating. An official marriage record existed at Tallulah, Louisiana. In his 1915 questionnaire, Abbs confirmed he was still living with Elizabeth and had never been previously married. Elizabeth, likewise, had no prior marriages.

The couple had at least four children:
- Mary Abbs (born approximately 1875)
- Caroline Abbs (born approximately 1876)
- Henry Abbs (born approximately 1880)
- A child identified as "Classtr" Abbs, who was deceased by 1915

After his discharge in 1866, Abbs spent approximately twenty years in East Carroll Parish, Louisiana, before moving to Bolivar County, Mississippi, where he spent the rest of his life. He lived at various addresses within the county—near Bolivar, at "Maxime," near Benoit, and finally at Stovall—working as a farmer throughout. The Mississippi Delta, where Bolivar County is located, was a region of large cotton plantations, and Abbs's movements likely followed the rhythms of tenant farming and labor arrangements.

Sometime after Elizabeth's death (the date is not recorded in the file), Abbs married Sallie Gordon on June 24, 1926—when he was approximately 83 years old. This late marriage would have significant consequences for his widow's pension claim.

In his later years, Abbs lived at Stovall, Mississippi, on land associated with the Stovall plantation. His pension checks were held at the local post office by C.B. Morris, the postmaster, who also served as the agent for "Mrs. Wm. H. Stovall, Merchant and Planter." Morris's 1928 letter to the Bureau reveals the paternalistic dynamics of life in the Mississippi Delta:

> "Old man Wilkins married Sally Gardner just about two years ago and I understand that she is applying through some agency, for his pension; personally I advised her to leave checks here in our safe untill she found out the correct thing to do."

## Outcome

Wilkins Abbs died at midnight on November 4, 1927, at Stovall, Mississippi. He was approximately 84 years old. At the time of his death, he was receiving $65 per month in pension payments—a rate that reflected both his extreme age and the cumulative effect of successive acts of Congress that had liberalized pension benefits for Civil War veterans.

His death triggered the cancellation of several pension checks that had been mailed but not cashed, and the Bureau promptly dropped his name from the pension rolls.

### The Widow's Claim

Sallie Gordon Abbs attempted to claim a widow's pension through the Coahoma County Chapter of the American Red Cross, with attorney J.H. O'Neal of Clarksdale, Mississippi, and later with the assistance of Red Cross secretary Nelly O'Brien and chapter chairman J.H. Johnson. The initial letter from J.H. Johnson in March 1930 erroneously stated that the marriage had occurred on June 24, 1905. When this was corrected to the actual date of June 24, 1926, the Bureau informed Sallie that she had no pensionable status.

Under the Act of May 1, 1920, a widow had to have married the soldier prior to June 27, 1905, to qualify for a pension. Sallie's 1926 marriage fell far outside this window. The Bureau was also careful to note that "the soldier alleged in a paper filed in this Bureau in 1915, that he was then living with his wife, Elizabeth Hopkins Abbs, whom he married in 1879"—a detail that further complicated any claim Sallie might have advanced.

The file was abandoned on April 22, 1930. Sallie Gordon Abbs, a dependent widow with "no property" living in rural Mississippi during the onset of the Great Depression, was left without any pension benefit from her husband's military service.

## Historical Notes

The pension file of Wilkins Abbs is a remarkably complete record that illuminates several dimensions of the Black veteran experience in post-Civil War America.

**The tyranny of documentation:** The central irony of Abbs's case is that a man who was enslaved—deliberately denied literacy, denied official records of his birth, denied the ability to accumulate the kind of documentation the state required—was then expected to produce precise dates, medical records, and documentary proof to claim benefits for his military service. He did not know his own birth year. He could not write his own name. Yet the Bureau demanded specificity: Was he 62 or 70? Did his hemorrhoids exist on August 29, 1904, or only on November 1, 1905?

**The role of community:** In the absence of official records, Abbs depended on his community—neighbors, fellow farmers, local doctors—to testify on his behalf. Men like John Logwood, Moses Green, Charles Scott, and O.O. Lewis provided the sworn testimony that substituted for the documentary record Abbs never had. These networks of mutual support were essential to Black veterans' pension claims, and they reveal the dense social fabric of post-emancipation Black communities in the Mississippi Delta.

**The plantation economy's persistence:** Even decades after emancipation, Abbs's life was entangled with the plantation system. He lived on or near plantations, his pension checks were held by a plantation postmaster, and his widow's pension inquiry was routed through an attorney and the Red Cross chapter in the nearby town. The postmaster's casual reference to "Old man Wilkins" and his proprietary handling of the pension checks speaks to the enduring structures of racial paternalism in the Deep South.

**The pension as lifeline:** By the end of his life, Abbs's $65 monthly pension was likely the most reliable income he had ever known. The fact that Sallie Gordon married him when he was 83 and she apparently had "no property" suggests the pension itself may have been a factor in their late union—though the human dimensions of their relationship cannot be reduced to economics alone.

**Multiple attorneys, multiple strategies:** Over three decades, Abbs was represented by at least four different law firms—Tucker, Evans & Manogue; Frederick E. Tasker; William Fletcher & Co.; and Cos. Altenberg—each taking their turn at navigating the Bureau's ever-shifting requirements. This revolving door of attorneys was common among pension claimants and reflected both the difficulty of the cases and the cottage industry that grew up around pension claims.

In the end, Wilkins Abbs did receive his pension, and he received it for nearly twenty years—from 1908 until his death in 1927. But the sixteen years of effort it took to achieve that result, the multiple rejections, the demands for proof he could not produce, and the ultimate denial of his widow's claim together constitute a record not just of one man's persistence, but of the systematic obstacles that Black veterans faced in claiming the benefits they had earned through their service to the Union.
