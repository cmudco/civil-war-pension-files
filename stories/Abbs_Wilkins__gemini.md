### Background

Wilkins Abbs was born into slavery in Wilkinson County, Mississippi. Like many formerly enslaved people navigating the post-war federal bureaucracy, he did not know the exact year of his birth. Over the course of his decades-long interaction with the United States government, his birth year was variously recorded as 1840, 1843, 1846, and 1848. However, an investigator who interviewed him later in life noted he was about twenty years old when the Civil War came to the Mississippi River Valley, placing his birth around 1843. 

Before the war, Abbs lived as a laborer and farmhand. In a 1905 questionnaire, he named his former enslaver as Douglass Cooper, who held him in bondage up to the moment of his military enlistment.

### Military Service

In the summer of 1863, the Union Army secured Vicksburg, Mississippi, opening the doors of emancipation and military service to thousands of enslaved men in the region. On July 27, 1863, Wilkins Abbs enlisted in the Union Army at Vicksburg. 

He was mustered in as a Private in Company I of what was initially organized as the 1st Louisiana Colored Infantry (African Descent). As the Union standardized its Black regiments, his unit was redesignated as the 9th Louisiana Infantry, and finally as the 5th United States Colored Heavy Artillery (USCHA). 

Abbs served for nearly three years. Military records show he was detached for recruiting service in early 1864, helping to bring more Black men into the Union ranks. He was hospitalized briefly for catarrh (respiratory inflammation) and diarrhea in 1864, but he was otherwise present for duty. 

His service was physically grueling. While stationed in Vicksburg, he served on the Provost Guard (military police). He also endured the intense physical labor expected of Heavy Artillerymen. He was honorably discharged at Vicksburg on May 20, 1866. 

### The Pension Claim

Abbs first filed for an invalid pension in the summer of 1892, under the Act of 1890, which allowed veterans to claim pensions for disabilities that hindered their ability to perform manual labor, regardless of whether the injuries were strictly service-connected. 

However, Abbs firmly attributed his physical breakdown to his time in uniform. He claimed a pension based on "misery in the Head & jaw bones," chronic backache, and injuries to his toes. In later applications, he would add heart trouble, lung conditions, and "disease of the rectum" (severe hemorrhoids) to his list of ailments, stating that he was wholly incapacitated from earning his living as a farmer. 

### The Bureaucratic Journey

The pension file reveals a staggering bureaucratic ordeal that stretched over twenty years before Abbs saw a single dollar. 

His initial 1892 claim lingered in the system for over a decade. He was subjected to medical examinations and forced to provide affidavits from neighbors. In 1904, the Bureau finally rejected the claim, stating he was "not ratably disabled at date of filing." 

Undeterred, Abbs immediately filed again in August 1904. This sparked another years-long battle. He complained of internal hemorrhoids and lumbago. The Bureau demanded he prove exactly when the condition originated and required medical evidence showing that the specific degree of disability existed on the precise date he filed the claim in 1904. Abbs, an illiterate farmer living in rural Mississippi, struggled to produce the exact sequence of medical paperwork the government required. In 1908, the Bureau rejected this claim as well, citing "insufficiency of the evidence."

Abbs's breakthrough only came when the laws changed. In 1907, Congress passed a law granting pensions based on age and length of service, rather than medical disability. Abbs filed under this act in January 1908. Because the government's own records proved he had served over 90 days and was over the age of 62, the bureaucratic barriers finally fell. His pension was approved in March 1908. 

He later attempted to claim a higher rate by alleging he was 70 years old in 1910, which the Bureau rejected because he could not prove his age. However, under the subsequent Act of 1912, his age and nearly three years of service were finally recognized, and his pension steadily increased for the rest of his life.

### Voices from the File

The file is rich with the dictated voices of Abbs, his comrades, and his neighbors, almost all of whom signed their testimonies with an "X." 

Abbs provided vivid, painful glimpses into his life as a soldier. Describing his back pain in 1896, he stated: *"On or about Jany 1865 in Vicksburg I was Carrying 40 rounds of Cartridges and Other Equipments. being young it injured my spine. Causing Constant misery in the back."* In 1904, he added that the injury occurred *"from Carrying Heavy Cartridge Box & Etc while in Service and while a growing Boy."*

He also detailed the origin of his foot and jaw pain: *"A short while before I was discharged in Vicksburg Miss. one night while serving on duty as 'Provost Guard' in attempting to arrest some malefactors And while running after them I fall in a Hole and seriously hurt my right foot."* As for his head pain, he testified that a *"U S Surgeon pulled Two teeth at one time and layed me up in hospital one month in Vicksburg."*

His neighbors testified to his declining health. John Logwood and Moses Green, neighbors in Mayersville, swore in 1892 that Abbs could not do even a quarter of the work of an able-bodied man, noting he was a *"Farmer not able to earn a living on account of back ach contracted in the war."*

The examining surgeons' reports provide a clinical, sometimes unvarnished look at his body. In 1905, they noted an accelerated heart rate, a *"bunion on joint due to ill fitting shoe,"* and a five-inch dragging scar on his right arm. They also noted his lifestyle, writing, *"applicant uses tobacco to excess."*

### Family & Personal Life

Following his discharge, Abbs lived for twenty years in East Carroll Parish, Louisiana, before settling in Bolivar County, Mississippi (in towns including Benoit, Mound Bayou, and Stovall), where he worked as a farmer. 

On January 14, 1879, he married Elizabeth Hopkins in Milliken's Bend, Louisiana. The ceremony was performed by Reverend Stable White. Together they had at least four children: Mary, Caroline, Henry, and Classtr (who died before 1915). In a 1915 questionnaire, Abbs confirmed he was still living with Elizabeth.

Elizabeth apparently passed away, because in the twilight of his life, Wilkins Abbs married again. On June 24, 1926, the octogenarian veteran married Sallie Gordon. They had barely a year together before Wilkins died on the night of November 4, 1927.

Following his death, Sallie attempted to claim a widow's pension. C.B. Morris, the local postmaster in Stovall, Mississippi, wrote to the government in 1928, holding Wilkins's final pension checks in his safe. Morris wrote: *"Old man Wilkins married Sally Gardner just about two years ago and I understand that she is applying through some agency, for his pension; personally I advised her to leave checks here in our safe untill she found out the correct thing to do."*

### Outcome

After being denied for nearly 16 years, Wilkins Abbs finally won his pension in 1908 at the rate of $12 per month. As he aged and pension laws became more generous to Civil War veterans, his rate steadily climbed: to $18 in 1912, $24 in 1913, $30 in 1918, and eventually reaching $65 per month prior to his death in 1927.

Sallie Abbs's outcome was less fortunate. The local American Red Cross chapter attempted to help her file a claim, initially making a typographical error stating she married Wilkins in 1905, before correcting the date to 1926. This correction doomed her claim. The Bureau of Pensions firmly informed her representatives that under the Act of May 1, 1920, a widow was only entitled to a pension if she had married the veteran prior to June 27, 1905. Sallie Abbs was denied any survivor benefits, and Wilkins's final, uncashed pension checks were voided and returned to Washington.

### Historical Notes

The file of Wilkins Abbs vividly illustrates the systemic barriers Black veterans faced in the pension system. Before the age-based pension acts of 1907 and 1912, veterans had to provide exact medical chronologies linking their present physical decline to specific wartime events or specific filing dates. For formerly enslaved men like Abbs—who could not read or write, who lacked access to continuous medical care, and whose bodies were worn down by a lifetime of manual labor and slavery—meeting the Bureau's standard of evidence was nearly impossible. 

The file also highlights the tragedy of federal marriage cut-off dates for widows. Designed to prevent young women from marrying elderly veterans solely for pensions, these arbitrary dates disproportionately harmed elderly Black women like Sallie Abbs, leaving them destitute after spending their own later years caring for dying veterans. 

Yet, the file also demonstrates profound resilience. Abbs, supported by a network of Black neighbors who placed their "X" marks alongside his, refused to give up his claim. He persisted against a distant, highly literate, and frequently adversarial bureaucracy for decades until the government finally recognized its financial debt to him.
